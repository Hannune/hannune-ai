A verdict without the source quote is not reviewable

By TaeHo Kim (Hannune)  ·  Published October 26, 2026

Photo by 2H Media on Unsplash

Something I noticed only after the first real bilingual obligation check ran: when a client came back two weeks later about item 47 specifically, I couldn't answer from the results file. I had to go find the original Korean clause, match it to the English paragraph, re-read both, and work out whether the verdict had been right. About fifteen minutes. There were several hundred flagged items from that batch.

The word-for-word translation thing took me longer to get right than I expected. My first instinct was to use a good fluent translation. That turned out to make things worse for review. If the Korean says "shall be completed within 30 days of written notice" and the fluent English rendering flows naturally, it's easy to miss that the English version of the contract dropped the time bound entirely. That difference is exactly what the checker flagged. A crib that follows the Korean structure preserves the awkwardness and makes the gap visible. The reviewer doesn't need to read Korean for that to work.

The other thing I kept doing wrong early on was shortening the excerpts because they looked cleaner in the spreadsheet. But a paraphrase of what the clause says is not useful for verification. Someone going back to item 47 three weeks later needs the actual sentence, not my description of it.

I also added the matched excerpt from the other language version to every row, which I should have done from the start. But the word-for-word translation was the part that took the most iteration. I'm still not fully happy with how the prompt handles clauses that are structured very differently between the two languages, where the Korean condenses three English sub-clauses into a single sentence. The crib translation gets awkward and sometimes the correspondence isn't obvious.
